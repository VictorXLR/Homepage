Weblog / 004 / Film & Silicon Valley

AirDrop a million financial analysts into San Francisco

After nearly three hours of You Can See Everything, I’m less interested in figuring out Elizabeth Holmes than in figuring out why anyone let her keep running Theranos.

Victor Popoola · November 28, 2026 · 3-minute read

Nearly three hours into You Can See Everything, the word I kept reaching for was psychopath. That’s my reaction to how cold Elizabeth Holmes comes across on screen, not a diagnosis. The word that stuck with me afterward was infantile. I expected to come away intimidated by Holmes. Instead, I kept wondering why so many people had treated someone who struck me as that immature as the most serious person in the room.

Nathan Fielder and Lance Oppenheim start with an odd proposition: Holmes invites a film crew into her life shortly before she goes to prison. What I like about the film is that all that access doesn’t settle the question of what to believe. Nearly three hours with someone can make you feel like you know them. That feeling isn’t evidence. It’s also, inconveniently, the sort of feeling a good pitch can give you in twenty minutes.

The men who should have told her no

I think a lot of the men around Holmes seriously failed her. I don’t mean they owed her more belief or more protection. Some of them had the standing to ask uncomfortable questions, insist on independent proof, or tell her she had no business running a blood-testing company the way she was running it. That would have been more useful to her than another vote of confidence. It would also have been more useful to the people whose blood was being tested.

Holmes was responsible for her choices. The men who lent her their names and their authority were responsible for theirs. I came away thinking she was in no condition to run that company, and somehow that didn’t stop anything. I want to know what her backers thought would be enough to stop it. What result would have made them say: no more money, no more rollout, no more tests until someone outside this company can show us that the thing works?

AirDrop the analysts

I have a fear of Silicon Valley. I build software for a living, so I understand the appeal of getting something in front of people before it’s perfect. If a button in my app doesn’t work, I can fix it tomorrow. If a doctor acts on a bad blood test today, the patient doesn’t get to roll back the decision. I’m afraid of a culture that knows the difference and still finds “we’ll fix it later” persuasive when the founder says it well enough.

A financial analyst can’t tell you whether a blood test works. A lab scientist can. But an analyst can ask how much revenue came from tests run on the machine you’re selling, who checked that number, and why the answers aren’t in the materials you sent over. Those are boring questions. I’d love for it to be a little harder to raise money without answering them.

So here’s my proposal: AirDrop a financial analyst into San Francisco. Actually, a million of them. Give them the raw spreadsheets and let them ask their follow-up questions before anyone gets to show the next slide.
